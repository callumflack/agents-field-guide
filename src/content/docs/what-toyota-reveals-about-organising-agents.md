---
title: What Toyota reveals about organising agents
description: Production flow, exposed problems and practical help.
---

> But reducing work-in-process is not the object. The object is to expose problems.

— Taiichi Ohno, recalled by Michikazu Tanaka in [“What I Learned from Taiichi Ohno,” p. 48 (PDF)](https://www.lean.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/birth-of-lean-ch2.pdf#page=30).

Toyota’s production system connects two principles: **just-in-time**, where actual need governs production, and **jidoka**, where a problem can stop production before defective work passes onward. Together they organise what starts, what moves between processes, and what happens when something goes wrong. [Toyota’s explanation](https://global.toyota/en/company/vision-and-philosophy/production-system/).

The quintessential takeaway:

**Arrange work so that difficulties become visible to people who can change the conditions causing them—and give those people the means to do so.**

The important movement is from **coping with the work to improving how the work is arranged**. Need gives the work direction; exposure reveals what prevents it; practical help makes improvement possible.

## Let need govern what starts

A process makes what the next process needs, when needed, in the required quantity. Producing extra because a machine has spare capacity creates inventory. It can keep one process busy while doing nothing to advance the finished product.

For agent work, another assignment is useful when its result can advance the work. If unresolved decisions or review capacity already prevent existing results being used, starting more agents may enlarge the queue. Connect what starts upstream to what can actually be used downstream.

## Make problems consequential

Large buffers allow one process to conceal another’s difficulties. Reducing them makes the difficulties affect the line. **The exposed difficulty becomes a responsibility to improve the process.** Otherwise, removing the buffer simply makes work harder. Tanaka describes this on pp. 47–48.

For agent work, discovering a mistaken shared assumption must affect the tasks that depend on it. Recording the discovery while those tasks continue leaves the problem intact. Pause the affected work, preserve what revealed the mistake, and resolve the assumption before producing more results from it.

## Connect exposure to practical help

A stoppage makes a problem visible. Someone must then investigate its cause and change the work. Tanaka describes supervisors helping resolve difficulties and management reconciling demands across departments (pp. 47, 67–68).

For agent work, a finding needs to reach someone able to act on it. Resolve what your task’s authority permits. Carry a remaining decision to its owner with the affected work and consequence clear. Routing every interruption to the human can increase their burden while individual agents appear autonomous.

## Capable agents need capable connections

Tanaka describes people stationed between machines to compensate for poor coordination (pp. 51–52). Increasing each agent’s autonomy can similarly leave the human supplying context, reconciling outputs, and deciding which result supersedes another.

Look at the work created between participants: reconstructing intention, reconciling assumptions, recovering agreements, and making a handoff usable. Some human participation—choosing what matters, exercising taste, reading deeply—is the point of the work. Other participation is required because the automated pieces do not fit together. Improve that arrangement.

## Let the organisation emerge from improving actual work

Tanaka describes procedures and automation criteria developing through practical difficulties over years (pp. 63–66). The difficulties reveal which divisions, connections, and tools are useful.

For agent work, let problems encountered in doing the work shape the setup. Collecting frameworks and designing around them can embed assumptions about work that nobody has yet understood. Learn from Toyota without requiring the setup to resemble a Toyota factory.

The quintessential question:

**What work must you keep doing because the agents don’t fit together?**
