Shape Up’s breadboards and fat-marker sketches make a solution specific enough to discuss while leaving room to design it. Vague feature names conceal disagreements; detailed mockups commit people to choices before they understand their cost. Ryan Singer describes this balance in [Principles of Shaping](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.1-chapter-02).

The takeaway for agent work:

**Choose a representation that exposes the decision you need to make, and leaves other decisions open.**

## What the circuit analogy contributes

Singer borrows the electrical breadboard: components wired together without a finished enclosure. Deciding that a device needs a light and knob differs from deciding their placement or materials. The analogy separates functional connections from physical arrangement. In Shape Up, it explains breadboarding’s abstraction; “circuit design” is not a third shaping technique. [Find the Elements](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.3-chapter-04#breadboarding).

## Breadboards expose what connects to what

A breadboard names **places** someone can navigate to, **affordances** they can act on—including information they read—and **connections** showing where actions lead. Words replace pictures. You can rehearse a use case and change its sequence without specifying where controls sit.

Singer’s Autopay example exposes a consequential ambiguity: does enabling future payments also pay today’s invoice? Moving the option into the existing payment flow resolves that ambiguity. The drawing helps discover a better interaction, rather than merely document screens. Singer walks through this in [Shaping the Work, from 08:21](https://uxdx.com/session/shaping-the-work/).

## Fat-marker sketches expose spatial relationships

When arrangement is the problem, words and connections leave out something essential. A fat-marker sketch shows rough grouping and placement; thick strokes prevent fine detail. Singer’s to-do groups example tests whether adding a button within each group breaks the list’s visual coherence.

It establishes the spatial relationships the idea depends on while leaving precise dimensions and styling open. Incidental details, such as a sidebar, can still become accidental commitments. [Find the Elements](https://basecamp.com/shapeup/1.3-chapter-04#fat-marker-sketches).

## Applying the distinction with agents

Ask for a breadboard when the uncertainty concerns what someone can do and what happens next. Ask for a fat-marker sketch when understanding the idea depends on seeing relationships on the page. State which choices the sketch establishes and which remain open.

Singer’s [later breadboarding skill](https://github.com/rjs/shaping-skills/blob/main/breadboarding/skill.md) extends the map to UI and code affordances, calls and returned data. That remains a connected workflow: a list of architectural components alone cannot explain how someone achieves an outcome.

[Take a breadboard from sketch to proof](/methods/breadboard-to-proof/) carries a chosen flow into implementation and observable checks. This reference explains the representations used to think through it. The agent applications here are adaptations; a convincing sketch does not establish working software.
