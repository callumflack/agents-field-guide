title: Production leveling
description: "Follow-on to Toyota: volume, mix, changeovers and buffers."
---

**Production leveling (heijunka)** spreads the quantity and mix of production over time. The aim is to meet demand while reducing the peaks and troughs passed between processes.

The takeaway for agent work:

**Before adding capacity, examine how the way you release work creates overload elsewhere.**

## The schedule can create the surge

Orders placed in large, infrequent batches can make relatively steady customer demand appear erratic upstream. A process then alternates between overload and idle capacity because of how work reaches it.

For agents, consider several tasks handed to parallel workers and returned together for review. The commissioning arrangement creates the review surge. Releasing usable work in smaller increments may reduce it; adding workers may enlarge it.

## Make smaller batches practical

Long runs of one product save changeovers but make other products wait and send uneven demands to suppliers. Reducing the time and cost of changeovers makes a more frequent mix economical.

For agents, examine what makes changing tasks expensive: reconstructing context, preparing an environment, or recovering an agreement. Reduce that cost before prescribing smaller batches. Frequent handoffs that each require reconstruction can increase the work.

## Ask what the buffer protects

A small stock of finished goods near shipping can absorb fluctuating orders while production and suppliers work steadily. Its location and purpose matter: inventory at one boundary can reduce inventory throughout the system.

This qualifies the [Toyota reference’s discussion of exposing problems](/references/what-toyota-reveals-about-organising-agents/). Examine whether a buffer absorbs a real fluctuation or conceals a recurring difficulty that needs fixing. For agent work, also examine whether queued results will remain useful while they wait.

The closing question:

**Which fluctuations come from the need itself, and which do we create by how we organise the work?**
